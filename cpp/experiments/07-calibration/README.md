**Experiment 14b (Phase 9): Calibrating Heston — Identifiability, Noise, and Model Misfit**

**Objective**  
A model is only useful for pricing and hedging once its parameters are fitted to market prices. That raises two separate questions. First, identifiability: when the model is right, does the fit recover the true parameters, or can different parameter sets fit equally well? Second, misspecification: when the market is generated by something the model cannot represent, how does the fit fail?

**Methodology**  
The objective is the root-mean-square of vega-weighted price errors, which approximates the implied-volatility RMSE without inverting Black-Scholes inside the optimizer. It is minimized with the engine's Nelder-Mead over unconstrained parameters (κ, θ, σ, v0 through exponentials, ρ through tanh).

*Part 1, identifiability.* A Heston surface is generated from known parameters (κ = 1.5, θ = 0.05, σ = 0.6, ρ = -0.65, v0 = 0.035): 9 strikes from 80 to 120 and maturities of 3 months, 1 year and 2 years, 27 quotes in all. Calibration starts from 8 random points spread over a wide region (κ 0.3 to 6, θ and v0 0.01 to 0.12, σ 0.1 to 1.5, ρ -0.95 to 0.2), once on the exact surface and once with independent noise of 0.2 vol points added to every quote.

*Part 2, misfit.* Heston is calibrated to the surface generated by the Phase 8 Merton jump-diffusion (20% diffusion vol, one jump a year, mean log-jump -10%, jump vol 15%): 9 strikes and maturities of 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 years.

C++ only: the calibration routine is tested against known surfaces in both test suites. The extreme parameter set found in Part 2 was re-priced with the independent Python pricer (scipy quadrature): implied vols agree with the C++ to within 4e-9 at every grid point, so the extreme fit is not a numerical artifact.

**Discussion**  
**With a full surface, Heston's parameters are identifiable.** All 8 starts, some far from the truth, converged to the same point. On exact data that point is the true parameter set (fit error 3e-8 vol points). Parameter trade-offs (between κ and σ, or κ and θ) are often reported in Heston calibration, typically when few maturities are available. Three maturities spanning 3 months to 2 years were enough to pin everything down here. The objective does not need multiple starts in this setup, though it is cheap insurance.

**Noise hits the parameters unevenly.** With realistic quote noise of 0.2 vol points, the fit error (0.15 vol points) sits a little below the noise level, as expected when five parameters absorb part of the noise, and the parameters move very differently. The current variance v0 (0.5%), the vol of vol σ (0.2%) and the correlation ρ (2%) are well determined. The long-run variance θ moves 1.5%, and the mean-reversion speed κ moves 8%. κ is determined by how the smile changes across maturities, which is a subtler feature of the surface than its level or slope. This matches what Phase 8 found for the OU process: speeds of mean reversion are the hardest parameters to estimate, whether from time series or from option prices.

**Fitting the wrong model produces a good fit with meaningless parameters.** Asked to reproduce a jump-driven surface, Heston drove itself to an extreme corner: κ = 7,340 and σ = 680 with almost no initial variance. In that regime the variance mean-reverts in minutes while being enormously volatile, so over any option maturity it behaves like a jump process rather than a smoothly varying volatility. Heston is imitating jumps. The overall fit looks respectable (0.29 vol points), but the parameters have lost their meaning: a Feller ratio of 0.002 makes Monte Carlo simulation almost impossible (Experiment 14a), and nobody could hedge with a vol of vol of 68,000%. The errors also have a clear pattern. They are largest at the shortest maturity (0.62 RMSE, up to 1.04 vol points at 0.1 years) and fall steadily to 0.01 at two years, which is exactly where Experiment 14a showed the two models' skews differ most. A fit statistic alone would not reveal this. Checking whether the fitted parameters are economically plausible, and where the residuals concentrate, is what exposes the misspecification.

**Limitations.** Synthetic surfaces only: real option quotes have bid-ask spreads, a term structure of rates and dividends, and only some strikes are liquid. Parameters were unconstrained (the extreme Part 2 solution would be ruled out by bounds in practice, at the cost of a worse fit). The vega-weighted price objective approximates implied-vol errors and is least accurate for deep out-of-the-money options.

**Conclusion**  
With a well-spread surface Heston calibration is well posed: every start finds the same parameters and recovers the truth from clean data. Under quote noise the mean-reversion speed κ is the weak spot. When the data come from a model with jumps, Heston still produces a reasonable-looking fit, but only by moving to degenerate parameters, and the short-maturity errors show where it falls short. That combination, plausible parameters and structured residuals, is the practical test of whether a calibrated model can be trusted. It is also the case for combining stochastic volatility with jumps.
